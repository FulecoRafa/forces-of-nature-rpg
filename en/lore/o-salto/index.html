The leap

Voss’s alarm went off with the soft sound of birdsong. Projections of bright suns lit up the ceiling of his cramped bachelor’s room. — Good thing they invented these ‘berenguenjonsons’ — he thought. — I love this city, but it’s really hard to tell day from night. He climbed out of the foamy plastic duvets and set his feet on the ceiling. He turned on the shower and took a hot bath; the water on the back of his neck and the hum of the heating coil woke him up completely.

Walking along the wall of his tiny two-room tower, he reached the kitchen, ate two slices of toast and set off for the workday. The light from the black hole’s photon ring pierced through the half-open door, flooding the house and staining the indigo-blue walls an intense red, almost yellowish; it nearly blinded him. It was a monotonous landscape, always red. It wearied Voss. But this would be a special day.

Along the path of cable-stayed metal bridges stretching out in every direction, Voss whistled happily. He had submitted his application to the research and development office to, at last, work in the field. He would leave behind that artificial, crimson landscape to see the greens and blues known only from books and reports. No more paperwork, no more closed offices; never again would he wake up in the red.

The great advantage of his home sitting exactly opposite the office was that he could simply jump to get there. Looking up, the round building — suspended by steel cables and with four entrances, like the points of a compass — sat exactly between him and the black hole at the center of the city. It was the only view he truly enjoyed. The accretion disk adorned the office as though it were the most imposing thing there, and not the supermassive giant that generated the energy sustaining everyone who lived in that fragile black egg. Voss looked to one side — no one; to the other, only an officer in the distance. He braced himself and then jumped with all his strength. He left the artificial gravitational field that kept him pinned to the ground and began to float toward the black hole. The sense of freedom he felt in those brief seconds was interrupted only by the officer’s desperate shouts:

— One day you’ll fall and die, Voss! — the officer yelled.

Quickly switching on his anti-gravity boots, he slowed down gently and landed without much trouble at the door of the Department of Planetary Analysis and Reclamation. Excited, he ran straight to the room of the Directorate of History and Strategic Forecasting. There, he and the other candidates waited patiently for the directors to announce the results of the applications. Who would be approved? Who would, at last, have the chance to visit Navajo?

Then the directors appeared, their chairs floating through the high hall. They were imposing figures, fully dressed in crimson uniforms, a sign of high rank. Voss still preferred his short green cloak. Dorok raised his arm and the room fell silent. He then announced:

— Dear analysts, I know you are eagerly awaiting the chance to visit our promised Navajo once again. For generations we have studied the course of the planet and of the societies that still inhabit it, searching for a chance to return. Your work, so far and still to come, is truly the most important there is in Penrose.

The speech was interrupted by Liliane:

— However, I’m afraid we have bad news for all of you. One of our agents returned a few minutes ago with word that a great war is taking place on the planet at this very moment. For that reason, we are forced to withdraw all level 1 and 2 agents from the planet. That includes all of you in this room today, as new agents.

Finally, Jéssica spoke:

— We remind you of our society’s values: Collaboration, Peace and Non-Interference. To uphold those values, we have made our decision: no new agent will be approved today. You will remain with us here in Penrose, working together so that, at another opportunity, we may safely return to Navajo.

In unison:

— So that one day we may return! — they all shouted.

The directors fell silent and began to leave the room. The others kept a funereal silence. Only Voss remained in the center; he said nothing, but his rage spoke louder than a thousand voices, and it turned him as red as the city. How could they do this to him? How could they abandon the planet once again? How could they be so inert? How could they keep him from fulfilling his dream? They were nothing but cowards, who would rather watch the planet destroy itself than reclaim the promised land. And he swore he would go back, even if he had to reach the Schwarzschild radius to do it. After all, the terminal portals to Navajo were right behind that door.

The room began to creak. Everyone stopped to listen to the iron structure groan. Some bolts popped out of place and people shielded themselves. A figure rose above the crowd, floating despite not wearing the directors’ elegant gravitational control platforms. His eyes glowed an intense crimson. The directors drew their gravitational anchoring devices, but it was already too late. With a gesture of his hands, Voss made the hall implode: arches crumpled, glass shattered, and people were flung out to float in space, falling through the event horizon. The terminal doors swung open. He advanced to the old moss-covered stone structure; it was beautiful, and the remnants of the portal still brought moisture to the air. What a fantastic feeling! Reaching the void guarded by the gray frame, he saw space fold: glimpses of great galaxies and solar systems, stardust and, finally, a planet — a damp cave where creatures he took to be bears slept calmly. Yes, bears! What creatures! Then he crossed the threshold with a smile. The portal closed behind him, leaving only the emergency sirens and the screams snuffed out in the vacuum of space.
